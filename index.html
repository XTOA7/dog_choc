How the calculator works

Chocolate contains theobromine and caffeine (methylxanthines), which dogs metabolize far more slowly than humans. The plasma half-life of theobromine in dogs is approximately 17.5 hours, compared with 6–10 hours in humans.[2] This tool converts the amount and type of chocolate your dog ate into an estimated theobromine dose in mg per kg of body weight, then maps that to a risk range used in peer-reviewed veterinary research.

UK veterinary guidance from the Royal Veterinary College (RVC) emphasises that the three essential pieces of information when assessing chocolate ingestion are the type of chocolate, the amount consumed, and the dog's weight — exactly the inputs this calculator uses.[4]

The risk bands below are based on dose thresholds reported in the veterinary literature:

  • Under ~20 mg/kg — mild signs such as agitation or vomiting may occur.
  • ~20–40 mg/kg — tachycardia, hyperthermia, and hypertension possible. Contact your vet.
  • ~40–60 mg/kg — arrhythmias, ataxia, tremors possible. Seek veterinary care.
  • Over ~60 mg/kg — seizures and coma reported. Emergency care recommended.
  • 100–200 mg/kg — approximately 50% mortality in reported cases.

Thresholds based on Weingart et al. (2021) and MSD Veterinary Manual,[2][1] corroborated by BSAVA's UK clinical summary.[5]

Chocolate types and methylxanthine content

Darker and more concentrated chocolate is far more dangerous. The total methylxanthine concentration of dry cocoa powder is approximately 800 mg/oz, unsweetened baking chocolate approximately 450 mg/oz, semisweet chocolate approximately 150–160 mg/oz, and milk chocolate approximately 64 mg/oz. White chocolate is a negligible source of methylxanthines.[1] A retrospective study of 156 dogs found that 28 of the 44 dogs with clinical signs had ingested dark or bitter chocolate.[2] The Kennel Club (UK) reinforces this: the darker and more expensive the chocolate, the higher the theobromine content.[6]

What the research says

A peer-reviewed retrospective study of 156 chocolate ingestion events in dogs (2015–2019) reported that 44 dogs developed clinical signs, with the most common being agitation (33), tremor (22), and vomiting (21). Sinus tachycardia was the most frequent clinical finding (28 dogs). After decontamination and symptomatic treatment, 43 of the 44 dogs survived, giving a mortality rate of less than 3%.[2] The study authors note that the type and amount of chocolate and the time of ingestion are important factors in assessing risk. BSAVA's summary of this study notes that clinical signs are typically reported within 1–4 hours of ingestion,[5] which is earlier than the 6–12 hours sometimes cited from US sources — this difference likely reflects variation in study populations and reporting methods.

UK data from the Veterinary Poisons Information Service (VPIS) shows that chocolate is the single most common poisoning inquiry, with milk chocolate alone accounting for 1,113 cases in the 2025 annual report.[8]

Frequently asked questions

My dog ate chocolate — what should I do first?

Note the type and amount, your dog's weight, and the time it was eaten. Call your veterinarian or a pet poison helpline immediately. In the UK, Animal PoisonLine operates 24/7 on 01202 509000 (this is a charged service).[7] Do not induce vomiting unless a vet tells you to.

How long until chocolate affects a dog?

Clinical signs typically occur within 6–12 hours of ingestion according to US sources,[1] though UK BSAVA guidance reports signs most commonly appearing within 1–4 hours.[5] Theobromine has a long half-life in dogs (approximately 17.5 hours), so symptoms can persist or worsen over time.[2]

What are the signs of chocolate poisoning?

Common signs include vomiting, diarrhea, restlessness, excessive thirst, and excessive urination. Higher doses can cause hyperactivity, tremors, racing heart, seizures, and coma.[1] The RVC lists elevated heart rate, vomiting, diarrhea, muscle tremors, and seizures as key signs requiring urgent veterinary attention.[4]

Is white chocolate dangerous?

White chocolate is a negligible source of methylxanthines, but its high fat and sugar content can still cause stomach upset or pancreatitis.[1]

Can a small amount be safe?

It depends on the dog's weight and the chocolate type. Use the calculator as a rough guide, but when in doubt, call your vet — it's always better to ask.

What about chocolate wrappers and foil?

The Kennel Club warns that chocolate foil wrappers and advent calendar packaging can cause intestinal obstruction if swallowed, so watch for signs of stomach upset even if the chocolate itself was a low-risk type.[6]
